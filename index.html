In brief

On October 7, 2023, Hamas-led fighters broke through the fence around Gaza and attacked towns, kibbutzim, army posts and a music festival in southern Israel. They killed 1,195 people, most of them civilians, and took 251 hostages.

Israel’s war on Gaza began the same day. In the 1,084 days since, the Gaza Ministry of Health has recorded 73,928 Palestinians killed and 175,030 injured. Of the dead identified by name, 30% were children. In the occupied West Bank, Israeli forces and settlers have killed 1,123 more Palestinians. 477 Israeli soldiers have been killed in the Gaza ground operation.

A ceasefire took hold on October 10, 2025. The killing slowed. It has not stopped.

This page tracks those numbers as sources publish them. Part one charts the toll: when, where, and who. Part two steps back a century, following the historian Rashid Khalidi’s account of how Palestine came to this.

The official Gaza toll has risen by — since the ceasefire. Only about a fifth of that is new killing. The rest are people killed before the ceasefire who are only now being counted: bodies pulled from the rubble, and deaths added to the record after the Ministry of Health’s committee verified them. Part two

The hundred years’ war

The numbers above begin in 2023. The Palestinian American historian Rashid Khalidi argues that the story begins in 1917, and that it is best understood not as an ancient feud between two peoples but as a colonial war waged against the Palestinians, backed at every stage by the great power of the day.

In The Hundred Years’ War on Palestine (2020), Khalidi divides that century into six “declarations of war.” What follows summarizes his account in our own words, with the numbers from each period. For the full argument, read the book.

Prologue · 1899

A letter to Herzl

In the name of God, let Palestine be left alone.

Yusuf Diya al-Din al-Khalidi, letter to Theodor Herzl, 1899

Khalidi opens with his own family. In 1899 his great-great-great-uncle, Yusuf Diya al-Din al-Khalidi, a former mayor of Jerusalem, wrote to Theodor Herzl, the founder of political Zionism. He wrote that he sympathized with the plight of the Jews of Europe, but that Palestine was already inhabited, and he urged Herzl, in the name of God, to leave it alone.

Herzl replied that Jewish immigration would bring prosperity to the country’s non-Jewish inhabitants. He did not address the people living there as a nation with rights of its own. For Khalidi, that exchange holds the century to come in miniature.

1917 – 1939 · The first declaration

A national home, promised by a great power

In November 1917, Britain promised the Zionist movement “a national home for the Jewish people” in Palestine, a country its armies had not yet finished conquering, where Arabs were roughly nine in ten of the population. The letter called that majority “the existing non-Jewish communities,” with civil and religious rights but no national ones.

~90%of Palestine’s population was Arab in 1917
~5,000Palestinians killed as Britain crushed the 1936–39 revolt
Read more

Britain wrote the promise into its League of Nations Mandate for Palestine in 1922 and opened the country to Jewish immigration. The Jewish share of the population rose from about a tenth to nearly a third by 1939.

In 1936 Palestinians launched a six-month general strike and then an armed revolt. Britain put it down over three years with mass arrests, collective punishment, house demolitions and executions. Khalidi estimates that about one in ten adult Palestinian men were killed, wounded, imprisoned or exiled. The Palestinians entered the 1940s with their leadership broken and their fighters disarmed.

Further reading: Balfour Declaration · 1936–39 Arab revolt

1947 – 1948 · The second declaration

Partition, and al-Nakba

In November 1947 the United Nations voted to partition Palestine, allotting more than half the land to a Jewish state when Jews were about a third of the population. Khalidi counts the vote, driven by the United States and the Soviet Union, as the second declaration of war. What followed Palestinians call al-Nakba, “the catastrophe.”

530+villages depopulated or destroyed

Between the partition vote and the armistice of 1949, Zionist militias and then the new Israeli army carried out a campaign, documented as Plan Dalet, that emptied more than half of Mandatory Palestine’s Arab population from their homes. At Deir Yassin, in April 1948, more than a hundred villagers were killed. Villages were razed. Survivors were barred from returning, and their land, homes and bank accounts were absorbed by the new state.

Palestinian scholars and UN experts increasingly describe the events since October 2023 as a second Nakba.

Further reading: Palestine Remembered · Decolonize Palestine · UNRWA · Palestine refugees

1967 · The third declaration

Occupation

In six days in June 1967, Israel took the West Bank, East Jerusalem and Gaza, along with Egypt’s Sinai and Syria’s Golan Heights. Hundreds of thousands of Palestinians were displaced, many of them refugees for the second time. The occupation that began that week has never ended.

For Khalidi, the third declaration was UN Security Council Resolution 242. It became the basis of every later peace plan, yet it never named the Palestinians, referring only to “a just settlement of the refugee problem.”

300,000+Palestinians displaced in 1967
700,000+Israeli settlers in the occupied West Bank and East Jerusalem today
Read more

Israeli settlement of the occupied territories began within months of the war. Under the Fourth Geneva Convention an occupying power may not transfer its own population into occupied territory, and the International Court of Justice has found the settlements unlawful. They have grown under every Israeli government since.

Further reading: Resolution 242 · Occupation of the West Bank

1982 · The fourth declaration

The siege of Beirut

In June 1982 Israel invaded Lebanon to destroy the Palestine Liberation Organization, and laid siege to West Beirut for ten weeks with American support. Khalidi, then teaching in Beirut, lived through it. After the PLO’s fighters were evacuated under a US-brokered deal, a Lebanese militia allied with Israel entered the Sabra and Shatila refugee camps, which Israeli forces had surrounded, and killed hundreds of civilians over three days.

~18,000killed in Lebanon by September 1982, most of them civilians (Lebanese estimates)
460 – 3,500killed at Sabra and Shatila; estimates vary widely
Read more

An Israeli commission of inquiry, the Kahan Commission, found that defense minister Ariel Sharon bore personal responsibility for failing to prevent the massacre. He resigned as defense minister and became prime minister in 2001.

Further reading: 1982 Lebanon War · Sabra and Shatila massacre

1987 – 1995 · The fifth declaration

Uprising, then Oslo

In December 1987 a mass uprising, the first intifada, spread from Gaza’s refugee camps across the occupied territories: strikes, boycotts, stone-throwing, and committees that ran daily life. It put the Palestinians back on the world’s agenda. Khalidi advised the Palestinian delegation at the peace talks that followed in Madrid and Washington.

He argues the 1993 Oslo Accords were the fifth declaration: a Palestinian Authority with limited self-rule, while Israel kept control of land, borders, water and Jerusalem, and settlement kept growing.

1,100+Palestinians killed in the first intifada, 1987–93 (B’Tselem)
~160Israelis killed in the same period (B’Tselem)
Read more

The accords deferred every core question (borders, refugees, Jerusalem, settlements) to talks that never concluded. In the seven years after the handshake, the number of Israeli settlers in the West Bank nearly doubled.

Further reading: First intifada · Oslo Accords

2000 – 2014 · The sixth declaration

Walls and a blockade

When talks collapsed in 2000, a second, far deadlier intifada followed. Israel began building a separation barrier, mostly inside the West Bank, which the International Court of Justice found unlawful in 2004. It withdrew its settlers from Gaza in 2005, and after Hamas won Palestinian elections in 2006, imposed a land, sea and air blockade on the Strip that has held since 2007.

Khalidi counts the wars on Gaza that followed, in 2008–09, 2012 and 2014, as the sixth declaration.

Of the Palestinians killed in 2014, the UN counted 551 children. Before October 2023, the 2014 war was the deadliest of the Gaza wars.

Further reading: Second intifada · Blockade of Gaza · 2014 Gaza war

2017 – now · After the book

The chapter still being written

Khalidi’s book closes in 2017. Since October 7, 2023, the Gaza Ministry of Health has recorded 73,928 Palestinians killed in Gaza, more than all the death tolls above combined. That count is where this page begins, and it is updated as sources publish.

Numbers differ, but the scale is not in doubt.

Notes & caveats

  • No figure is complete. Gaza MoH itself states thousands of dead remain under rubble. Peer-reviewed research published in The Lancet Global Health estimated 75,200 violent deaths through January 2025 — roughly 35% higher than the MoH count for the same period — and did not capture deaths from starvation, disease, or collapsed healthcare. As of September 24, 2026, Gaza MoH reports 73,928 killed — including 1,400+ since the October 2025 ceasefire — and Wikipedia’s conflict tracker cites 75,131+ Palestinians killed across Gaza and the West Bank; excess non-violent deaths likely push the true toll substantially higher.
All notes and sources
  • Some category counts are no longer updated. MoH’s children (20,179) and women (12,500) breakdowns, medical personnel (1,701), and journalists (262) have not changed in months; starvation deaths (463) and aid-seekers killed (2,615) were last reported in mid-2026. The Committee to Protect Journalists, after a September 2026 review, counts 191 journalists killed, using a narrower definition.
  • The age and sex breakdown comes from the Ministry of Health’s list of dead identified by name, which trails the overall total.
  • OCHA relies on MoH for Gaza but independently verifies West Bank incidents. Its counts include fatalities from both Israeli military and settler attacks.
  • B’Tselem publishes a conservative, named-only database of verified individuals. Its totals trail MoH but document each death in detail.
  • Israeli soldier deaths are taken from IDF press releases and reporting by The Times of Israel. The count shown is soldiers killed in Gaza ground operations since October 27, 2023.
  • The governorate breakdown shown is a model, not a reported statistic. It distributes the overall Gaza total by pre-war population weighted for reported damage — directional only.
  • The dashboard is rebuilt from the Tech for Palestine API (which ingests OCHA flash updates and MoH briefings). Re-running the build pulls the latest daily data.
